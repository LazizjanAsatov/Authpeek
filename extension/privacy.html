Privacy policy

Last updated 25 November 2026 · applies to AuthPeek 1.3 and later

AuthPeek is a two-step verification (authenticator) extension for Chrome. It has no servers and no accounts of its own: what it keeps lives in your browser profile and, if you use sync, in your browser's sync storage (Chrome sync with your Google account), with your accounts and whitelist encrypted by your password. This policy says exactly what it handles, where that goes, and how to remove it. See also the terms of use.

Your AuthPeek password itself is never stored anywhere. Nobody, including the developer, can recover it or your codes without it.

What AuthPeek reads on web pages

  • On every page: AuthPeek looks at the page's form fields to notice a verification-code box, and remembers a username you type into a sign-in field (see the table above).
  • Only on pages with a verification-code box: it also reads usernames already filled into the page and email addresses shown in the page's text (for example "Signed in as you@example.com"), to tell apart several accounts on the same site. These are used on the spot and not saved.
  • Only when you click "Scan this page": it takes a picture of the visible part of the tab to find a QR code, and reads the page's text and fields for an otpauth:// link or a printed setup key. The picture and text are processed in your browser and discarded.
  • It never reads or saves anything else from pages, and it never fills a code by itself on a website you haven't linked to that account. If you choose to fill an account's code on another site (Fill in AuthPeek, or the right-click menu), it fills it there and links that site to the account. A code is only filled into a frame on the site it was picked for, never into an embedded frame from another site.

Network requests AuthPeek makes

AuthPeek talks to no server of its own. It makes only these ordinary web requests, which carry none of your accounts, codes or keys:

  • Clock check: each time you open AuthPeek while it is unlocked or unlock it, when you turn the clock check on in Settings, each time it fills a code, and each time it offers a code on a page, a request to Cloudflare's public time service (www.cloudflare.com/cdn-cgi/trace), because a wrong clock makes every code wrong and a laptop's clock can change at any time. You can turn this off in Settings.
  • Whitelisted IPs: if you set up a list, before showing, filling, sharing or exporting codes AuthPeek asks Cloudflare (www.cloudflare.com and 1.1.1.1, at /cdn-cgi/trace) for this computer's public IP. While things work normally it asks again at most every 30 seconds; a failed check, "Check again" or a change to the list asks right away. While the list is empty, the Whitelisted IPs section in Settings asks the same way, only to show you your IP so you can add it. The answer is compared with your list on your computer and kept only in memory.
  • Your internal address: an internal https:// address you add to the list is contacted directly from your browser to see if it answers. Redirects are not followed.

Service logos and the Inter font are bundled with AuthPeek, so showing them makes no request. Like any website you visit, Cloudflare and your internal server see your IP address and that a request was made. Cloudflare's own privacy policy applies to its side.

Sharing codes

You can share one account's codes with someone for 2, 5 or 10 minutes. AuthPeek works out those few minutes of codes on your computer, encrypts them with a new random key (AES-256-GCM), and puts both into a link. Besides the codes, the link carries the account's name as you saved it (for example GitHub (alice@example.com)), so the person you send it to can see which account it is, plus when the codes start, how long each lasts and how many digits they have. It never contains your secret key.

  • The key sits after the # in the link, and browsers never send that part to any server. The link opens a page hosted on GitHub Pages (lazizjanasatov.github.io), so GitHub sees that the page was requested, like any website, but receives nothing it can read. The page shows the codes from the link itself and makes no further request after it loads. The link can also be pasted into AuthPeek.
  • Anyone who has the link can see the codes until they run out; after that it shows nothing. Send it only to someone you trust.
  • AuthPeek doesn't keep the link or send it anywhere. Where it goes is up to you (copy, QR code, or your computer's share menu).
  • When Whitelisted IPs is on, links are only made on a whitelisted network.
  • When someone sends you a link, the codes are shown straight from it: AuthPeek doesn't save them or add the account, and you don't need to unlock AuthPeek to view them.

Google and sync

If sync is on in Chrome, Google (Chrome sync) carries AuthPeek's synced storage (the encrypted accounts and whitelist, and the plain settings) to your other computers. Google can't read the encrypted parts without your password. Google's own terms and privacy policy apply to sync.

Backups and exports

  • Backup files and backup text are made on your computer and encrypted with your password. You decide where they go. Backups contain your accounts, not your whitelisted IPs list.
  • "Export as links" shows your accounts as plain otpauth:// links after you enter your password. That export is not encrypted and is never saved unless you choose to.
  • "Move to another app" shows one account's otpauth:// link and its QR code on your screen, after you enter your password, so a phone can scan it. That link contains the secret key. It is shown only when you ask for it, removed from the screen when you leave that view, and never saved.
  • When Whitelisted IPs is on, backups, exports and the setup QR code only work on a whitelisted network.
  • Copying or filling a code puts it on your clipboard or into the page you chose.

What AuthPeek never does

  • No analytics, tracking, advertising, profiling, or selling or sharing of data.
  • No data is sent to the developer or to any third party beyond the requests listed above and the share links you choose to send.
  • Data is used only to provide AuthPeek's features, never for anything unrelated. AuthPeek's use of data complies with the Chrome Web Store User Data Policy, including the Limited Use requirements.

Why it asks for each permission

  • Read and change data on websites: to notice a verification-code box on a sign-in page, fill the code in, and make the network requests above.
  • Storage: to keep your encrypted accounts, whitelist and settings, and the short-lived memory described above.
  • Context menus: to add "AuthPeek" to the right-click menu of text boxes. While AuthPeek is unlocked the menu lists your account names; it never shows codes or keys.
  • Active tab: to take the picture of the page for "Scan this page", only when you click it.

Whitelisted IPs is a guardrail

The whitelist keeps codes off networks you haven't approved. It is not a lock against someone who knows your AuthPeek password: they can still move your keys to another app.

Removing your data

Delete accounts in AuthPeek, or uninstall it. Uninstalling removes AuthPeek's data from this computer. To remove the synced copy as well, delete your accounts first, or clear synced data in your Google account's Chrome sync settings. Memory-only data is gone when the browser closes. A share link you sent stops showing codes once they run out.

Children

AuthPeek is not directed at children and does not knowingly collect information from anyone.

Changes to this policy

If AuthPeek starts handling data differently, this policy is updated before that version is released and the date above changes. AuthPeek is open source, so you can check everything this policy says in its code.
